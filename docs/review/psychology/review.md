# Psychology review — Krit prototype gallery

## Top-line verdict

The four prototypes share a writer's voice that is unusually well-calibrated for adult learners — concrete, loss-framed, falsifiable, allergic to platitudes — and that voice is the single biggest motivational asset across the set. Where the artifacts win, they win because they manufacture a *curiosity gap* (Loewenstein) and then close it with predict-then-reveal so the reader experiences a positive reward prediction error (Schultz). Where they lose, they lose by *front-loading friction*: calibration quizzes, dense abstracts, and wall-of-text essay openings burn working memory before any payoff has arrived (Fogg's B = MAT — motivation drops faster than ability rises). The gallery feels like a magazine of cleverly written essays with embedded toys, not a curriculum — which is a compliment for one-off reads, and a warning for an LMS that needs Day-2 return.

## Per-prototype attention/motivation arcs

### 1. Embeddings (tech) — SDT: autonomy 7, competence 8, relatedness 5

**First 5 sec.** Eye lands on "Embeddings", then the kickers (`14 CHECKPOINTS · 4 PREDICT MOMENTS`) — *competence promises* under SDT that work: the reader knows the road. The chapter rail and `0 PTS` pill are visible from t=0, gamifying upfront. Risk: telegraphing the structure removes Zeigarnik tension (open loops drive return). **Middle 80%.** The chapter-gate model (`Lock in chapter N`) is the strongest sustained-attention engine in the set — each gate is a Zeigarnik close. The spray-burst plus C-major-arpeggio chime (`playChime` in source: square-wave, fixed per-chapter pattern) is *fixed-schedule reinforcement*, not variable-ratio: every gate fires the same shape of jingle. Good for pacing, weak for dopamine — variable-ratio is what hooks slot-machine brains (Skinner). **Friction/reward.** Predict-then-reveal cards exploit the testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke) and are excellent. The Layer 1 calibration quiz at the top, however, is competence-threat *before any payoff*: it asks the user to declare ignorance before earning a dopamine hit. Move it to after Chapter 1. **Chiptune verdict.** Square-wave arpeggios feel earned, not contrived, because the rest of the design is loud (graffiti, points). They'd feel adolescent if typography were quiet. The `♪` mute is the right escape valve.

### 2. Compound interest (finance) — SDT: autonomy 4, competence 7, relatedness 8

**First 5 sec.** "The cost of waiting" in gold on navy is the most emotionally loaded hero in the set. Loss framing doubles attentional pull versus gain (Kahneman & Tversky 1979). "₹7 crore vs ₹3 crore" anchors a number the reader cannot help imagining themselves into. **Middle 80%.** The commit-slider → simulate → reveal arc is a textbook flow loop (Csikszentmihalyi): single goal, clear feedback, replaceable difficulty via counterfactual replay. The gold age-65 reveal — 1.6s count-up with gradient sweep — is the most successful single moment in the gallery. It delivers because the reader *earned* it by committing first; without the commit it would feel like an infographic. Replay buttons reuse the same animation (good Hick's-law reduction, no new affordance) but the prediction-error magnitude shrinks on each run (habituation). **Friction/reward.** Excellent: single gate, huge payoff, one-click replay. **Bounce risk.** Autonomy is low — guided rail, no off-ramp for a reader who doesn't care about retirement at 22.

### 3. Positioning (marketing) — SDT: autonomy 6, competence 6, relatedness 7

**First 5 sec.** "Same drink, different question." in claret italics is the prose equivalent of the bicycle headline — concrete, anti-cliché. **Middle 80%.** The most essay-heavy artifact, and where attention is most at risk: two scrolls of body text before the first interactive element. The "Reveal answer" buttons are *desirable difficulty* (Bjork) in theory but in practice gate only the answer, not the prediction — most readers will scan the question, think for half a second, click. That's a click tax, not a learning event. Add a one-line prediction input before the reveal and the move becomes productive. The vagueness-flagging build-your-own is the best interaction in the set: real-time, opinionated, generative. **Friction/reward.** Reward is intellectual ("I see it now"); no dopamine architecture — no points, animations, count-ups. Fine for the marketing-curious; flat affect for an LMS at scale.

### 4. Bicycle (mechanics) — SDT: autonomy 5, competence 9, relatedness 7

**First 5 sec.** "Why a bicycle *stays up*." in claret italics is *productive cognitive dissonance*: the reader feels the answer is obvious (gyroscopic forces) and the subtitle says they're wrong. Strongest hook in the gallery — it activates System 2 by violating an expectation the reader didn't know they had (Kahneman). **Middle 80%.** "Demolish the wrong answer first" is the riskiest move in the set; carelessly used it's adversarial and patronising. Used here, with a *predict-then-falsify commit* (the three-option button row that records the guess before showing Kooijman's result), it converts dissonance into ego-investment. The reader who picks the wrong answer (most do) is now emotionally bound to the resolution — the cognitive-affective interplay that drives consolidation (Mayer 2009). **Friction/reward.** The Abstract is the only real weakness — ~150 words of academic prose between the dissonance headline and the predict button. Cut it to two sentences or hide it under a TL;DR disclosure.

## Dopamine architecture across the set

Three of four prototypes use **predict-then-reveal** as their primary dopamine engine — the right primitive: prediction commits the reader's identity to an answer, the reveal generates a real reward prediction error, the gap drives consolidation (Schultz; testing effect). The architecture's weakness is that prediction-error is **fixed-schedule** across the gallery — the reader knows a reveal is coming every time they commit. Variable-ratio reinforcement is what creates compulsive return (Skinner). Krit currently has **zero variable-ratio surprise**; every reward is telegraphed by the button label. Embeddings comes closest to compensating, with jittered SVG spray paths plus per-chapter unique jingle, but the *occurrence* is still deterministic.

What fires where: **awe** — the gold-₹7-crore age-65 reveal (finance), the "bike stayed up" reveal (mechanics); **delight** — spray-burst+chime at each embeddings chapter close; **recognition** — cosine drag-vector, contrastive force sim, build-your-own diagnostic; **frustration** — mostly absent, which is itself a problem since frustration-that-resolves is sticky (Sweller's productive load). The bicycle predict-commit is the only place a reader is allowed to be wrong on the record.

Missing: **anticipation between sessions.** No prototype plants a hook that pulls the reader back tomorrow. No streak, no daily-released chapter, no SRS (spaced-retrieval) ping, no "you scored X today, peers averaged Y". LMS table stakes — Krit's prototypes read as one-shot magazine pieces, not week-long courses.

## Where would a representative reader bounce?

1. **Embeddings, the Layer 1 calibration quiz at the top.** Five Likert questions before any payoff. Cause: **competence threat without prior autonomy** (SDT) — asked to declare ignorance before earning agency. Self-efficacy dips; Fogg's next-action threshold is unmet.
2. **Marketing, second screen of unbroken essay before the first reveal-button.** ~400 serif words. Cause: **habituation / von Restorff deficit** — visual field unchanged, System 1 stops detecting signal. Bounce probability spikes around the 90-second dwell mark.
3. **Bicycle, the academic Abstract.** 150 words between the dissonance headline and the predict-commit. Cause: **present-bias mismatch** (Ainslie) — the headline-aroused reader wants resolution NOW; lit-review scaffolding feels like the wrong delay.
4. **Embeddings, late chapters (5–8) where the chiptune becomes predictable.** Cause: **dopamine habituation under fixed-schedule reinforcement** (Schultz). First three jingles surprise; the next five don't. Reward signal flattens as cognitive load climbs.
5. **Finance, after the first replay.** Cause: **reduced prediction-error magnitude on repeated reveal.** Same count-up, smaller surprise. Reader has the gist after run 2.

## Top 3 psychological changes to improve sustained engagement

1. **Move the embeddings calibration quiz to a Layer-3 "Reflect" slot at the end.** A competence self-assessment after the user has earned points and survived chapter gates flips it from threat to validation (SDT autonomy + competence both rise) and gives a *before/after* contrast that closes a Zeigarnik loop opened at the start.
2. **Introduce one variable-ratio surprise per prototype.** Every reveal is currently predictable. Add a ~15%-probability "bonus insight" card on chapter close in embeddings; an occasional unexpected counterfactual on the finance simulate; a surprise "common misconception you might have had" reveal on bicycle. Variable-ratio reinforcement (Skinner; Schultz) is what converts a magazine read into a habit.
3. **Plant a between-session hook.** An LMS lives or dies on Day-2 return; these prototypes have no Zeigarnik thread extending past page-close. End every prototype with one unresolved question as a "tomorrow we'll answer…" card; show a streak counter beside the points pill; on return, lead with the user's prior commitments ("you predicted 0.4, the actual was 0.73 — let's go further"). Self-referential recall is one of the strongest known retrieval cues (Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker 1977).
