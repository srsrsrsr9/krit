### Speech block 1 — *Opening: the thermostat in your skull*  *(target 0:00 – 1:20)*

[Indian-English accent, calm Bengaluru lecturer tone] [matter-of-fact] You assume good things keep feeling good. [pause] They do not.

[dry] Your brain has, hardwired into it, a happiness thermostat. And the thermostat is annoyingly competent.

[wry] This lesson is the bad news that becomes good news. Bad — nothing stays special. Good — nothing stays awful either. Same plumbing makes both true.

[firm] By the end of this lesson, you'll have one mental model — the thermostat — and two named moves. *The adaptation curve.* And *variety beats intensity.* Together they explain why your last big purchase already feels boring, and what you can do about it that doesn't involve buying another one.

[thoughtful] Quick reminder. In lesson one we said your brain forecasts the future from two frames. Today we're staying in the present and the recent past — the days and weeks *after* the thing already happened. The thermostat is what does the work in those weeks. [pause] Once you see it, you'll spot it everywhere.

### Speech block 2 — *Mental model: the happiness baseline*  *(target 1:20 – 3:20)*

[curious] Here's the picture. [pause] Think of happiness like a thermostat set somewhere around seven out of ten.

[matter-of-fact] External events push the dial briefly. Up, when good things happen — a new job, a vacation, biryani. Down, when bad things happen — a breakup, food poisoning, a passive-aggressive Slack message.

[firm] But the thermostat doesn't *care* about the events. It cares about the setpoint. After every push, it quietly pulls the dial back to seven.

[gentle] This is a feature, not a bug. If the thermostat didn't reset, your last breakup would still be running your life. So would your first crush. So would the moment you got into college. [dry] So would the worst exam result you've ever received. The thermostat is the reason you can function at all.

[firm] Most of self-help is built on the belief that you can move the dial. You can. For a few weeks. [emphatic] Almost nothing in the standard self-help catalogue moves the *setpoint.* And the setpoint is the only number that matters in the long run.

[thoughtful] A useful diagnostic. Whenever you make a change in your life, ask yourself: am I trying to move the dial, or move the setpoint? Most people are trying to move the dial without realizing it, and getting frustrated when the dial doesn't stay where they pushed it. [matter-of-fact] The dial isn't *supposed* to stay there. The setpoint is supposed to stay there.

[gentle] Today is mostly about understanding the dial. Setpoint changes get covered in lesson four.

### Speech block 3 — *Move 1: the adaptation curve*  *(target 3:20 – 6:20)*

[firm] Move one. The adaptation curve.

[matter-of-fact] Here's the most cited finding in the field. Phil Brickman and colleagues, nineteen seventy-eight. They compared three groups: recent lottery winners, recent paraplegics from accidents, and a control group of ordinary people. They followed all three for eighteen months.

[emphatic] What they found was striking. *All three groups reported similar happiness levels at the eighteen-month mark.* [pause] The lottery winners had adapted up. The paraplegics had adapted up too — though, importantly, from a much lower temporary trough. Both extremes had returned toward baseline.

[curious] This is what we mean by the adaptation curve. Take any positive event. Plot mood over time. You get a sharp spike right after the event, fast decay through the first month, and a near-complete return to baseline by month two or three.

[dry] A boring number. A one-lakh-rupee phone increases self-reported happiness for roughly fourteen days before the upgrade becomes "the phone." By month three, it's actually generating *negative* affect when the battery dies, when the screen smudges, when a newer model launches.

[matter-of-fact] The decay is fastest in the first thirty days. After about day forty-five, you're at pure utility — the thing exists for you only as a tool. After day ninety, it's a mild irritant if it has any flaws, and you're already noticing the next model.

[firm] What this means in practical terms. The bumps you get from external upgrades are real. They are also short. And the curve is much steeper than your brain predicted. [wry] If you're making a financial decision based on "I'll feel this way for a long time," you should mentally divide the duration by ten and re-do the math.

[switching to Hinglish, warm] Pehla week ka AC: "yaar zindagi badal gayi." Doosra mahine ka AC: "thoda zyada cool karna chahiye." Teesra mahine ka bill: "yaar AC band karte hain raat ko." [chuckles] Chautha mahine: "AC nahi hai matlab insaan kaise rehte hain bhai?"

[returning to English, dry] You adapted to the AC in both directions. First the novelty faded. Then the absence became intolerable. Same plumbing, opposite outcomes.

### Speech block 4 — *Move 2: variety beats intensity*  *(target 6:20 – 9:20)*

[firm] Move two. If adaptation is the enemy, the cheap counter is *variety.*

[matter-of-fact] Five different small pleasures — coffee with a friend on Tuesday, a four-kilometer walk on Wednesday, a new playlist on Thursday, a half-hour with an old book on Friday, a board game on Saturday — consistently beat one expensive intense pleasure, like a five-star dinner once a month, on weekly happiness measures.

[curious] Why? Because each new context partially resets the adaptation clock. Your brain is bad at adapting to a moving target.

[wry] Variety is hedonic arbitrage at zero marginal cost. Structurally cheaper than intensity. Structurally more sustainable. Structurally more interesting. [emphatic] Three out of three.

[firm] There's a hierarchy worth memorizing. From worst to best.

[slowly] Routine. Almost no anti-adaptation effect. Pure habit; the brain has fully adapted. Same coffee at the same place at the same time, every morning.

[matter-of-fact] Then, intensity. The instinct most people fall into when routine starts feeling stale. *Bigger* coffee, *fancier* coffee, *more* coffee. This produces a one-cycle bump and then your hedonic budget collapses, because the new intensity becomes the new routine.

[curious] Then, variety. Rotate between four different coffee shops on different days. Now your brain has four moving targets to adapt to, and it's slower to settle on any of them.

[firm, slowly] Then, at the top of the hierarchy — *deprivation.* Skip the coffee for a week, then return to it. The reward system re-sensitizes. You taste the coffee. You notice the coffee. The first cup back is, briefly, the best coffee you've had in months.

[brisk] Routine, then intensity, then variety, then deprivation. Bottom to top. [dry] Most people stop at intensity, which is the most expensive way to be only briefly happier.

[wry] There's a name for what happens when you keep climbing the intensity ladder. Call it the *intensity arms race.* You feel the bump fading. You instinctively double the dose. The bump returns, briefly. It fades faster than last time. You double again. Within a few cycles you've spent significantly more money for significantly less effect.

[firm] The fix is mechanical. *Don't chase the dragon. Rotate the dragons.* Variety beats intensity, every single time the experiment runs.

[switching to Hinglish, warm] Hostel jaane se pehle: "yaar mummy ke haath ka khana boring hai, daily wahi sambhar." Hostel ke teen mahine baad — [emphatic] "Bhai, mummy ka sambhar heaven hai!"

[returning to English, dry] Deprivation equals re-sensitization. Same sambhar, different thermostat reading. This is science, not sentiment.

### Speech block 5 — *Try it and reflect*  *(target 9:20 – 12:00)*

[warm] Time to run the experiment on yourself.

[firm] Pick one daily pleasure you currently take for granted. Coffee, your evening walk, your favorite show, your weekend brunch place — anything you do on autopilot.

[matter-of-fact] Now design a seven-day experiment that uses variety or deprivation to re-sensitize you to it. Be specific about days.

[curious] Here's a concrete example. Let's say you currently watch one episode of Brooklyn Nine-Nine every evening on autopilot. Days one through four — skip entirely. Replace with reading. Day five — watch with full attention, no phone in the room. Days six and seven — rotate to two different new shows on alternating evenings. Then on day eight, return to your original.

[wry] What you'll typically find. The show you watched without thinking for years suddenly feels enjoyable again. Not because the show changed. Because your brain re-sensitized to it.

[firm] That's the entire move. Run that on one thing for one week and you'll know it works.

[brisk] Quick recap. Move one was the adaptation curve. The decay is steepest in the first thirty days; you're back to baseline by day sixty for most things. Move two was variety beats intensity, with deprivation at the top of the hierarchy and the intensity arms race as the trap most people fall into.

[firm] Five takeaways. [pause] One. Hedonic adaptation is plumbing, not philosophy. You cannot will it away. Two. Variety beats intensity, and deprivation beats variety. Three. The intensity arms race is the most expensive way to be only briefly happier. Four. Symmetric adaptation is also why most bad days fade faster than you fear. Five. The strongest single re-sensitizer is short, bounded deprivation.

[gentle] The reflection prompt. *Where in your life are you currently chasing intensity instead of variety?* [pause] Take a minute on that one.

[warm] We'll see you in lesson three. Where we tackle the most uncomfortable problem in this whole field — *why knowing all of this changes almost nothing about your behavior.* [wry] The G.I. Joe Fallacy is coming.
