### Speech block 1 — *Opening: your brain is a bad GPS*  *(target 0:00 – 1:30)*

[Indian-English accent, calm Bengaluru lecturer tone] [thoughtful, slightly amused] You think you know what will make you happy. [pause] You are usually wrong.

[matter-of-fact] Not in a charming, philosophical way. Wrong in a measurable, repeatable, fund-the-research way. The mistake follows patterns; and once you see the patterns, you cannot unsee them.

[warm] By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to look at your own *want list* and tell which items are signal, and which are noise.

[dry] Welcome to the most expensive bug in your operating system.

[matter-of-fact] We have two named moves to install today. The first is *miswanting* — the mismatch between what you predict will make you happy, and what actually does. The second is the *arrival fallacy* — the special case of miswanting that hits you around big finish lines. Promotions. Admissions. Weddings. [pause]

[firm] Quick framing before we go in. Nothing here is going to tell you to stop wanting things. Wanting is a perfectly fine engine. The problem is that the destination your brain picks for the engine is usually the wrong one.

[wry] By the end of this lesson, you'll have a single diagnostic question that can save you, conservatively, a few promotions and at least one international move.

### Speech block 2 — *Mental model: the wedding photographer*  *(target 1:30 – 3:40)*

[thoughtful] Picture this. [pause] Your brain is a wedding photographer. Not a great one. The card slot in their camera is empty.

[curious] They show up to every event in your life — a vacation, a job offer, a breakup — and they take exactly two photos. The first is the *peak* of the event. The second is the *end*. [pause] That's it.

[matter-of-fact] Everything in between, the long middle of every experience, gets dropped on the cutting-room floor. There is a wastebasket beside the photographer; that is where it goes.

[firm] So when your brain forecasts the future — when it tells you, *the trip will feel amazing,* or *the meeting will be excruciating,* or *the new job will be life-changing* — it is making that forecast from two frames. [pause] Just two.

[dry] Two frames out of, conservatively, eight thousand. The other seven thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight become the future you forgot to plan for.

[gentle] The implication is uncomfortable. Your forecasts about how the future will feel are systematically wrong. Not randomly. *Systematically.* They overweight the emotional peaks. They underweight the long flat middle. They give you a highlight reel and call it a documentary.

[warm] This is true for big events and small ones. True for things you're excited about and things you're dreading. True for what you imagine your life will be after a finish line. The photographer is the same.

[thoughtful] The good news is, knowing this is half the fix. The other half — actually doing something about it — comes in lesson three. For now, just start carrying the photographer with you.

### Speech block 3 — *Move 1: miswanting*  *(target 3:40 – 6:50)*

[firm] The first named move is called *miswanting.* Coined by Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson, two psychologists at Harvard and Virginia, in two thousand and three. Verified across decades on lottery winners, salary jumps, and breakups.

[matter-of-fact] Miswanting is wanting things that, on arrival, do not move you the way you predicted. [emphatic] Note carefully — the bug is not in the wanting. The bug is in the prediction about what the thing will do for you when you get it.

[curious] A crisp example. Suppose you're an engineer in Bengaluru, currently making fifteen lakh rupees a year. You're being courted by a competitor who'll pay you thirty-five. A twenty-lakh jump. You imagine the new salary, the calmer apartment, sleeping deeper, finally — *finally* — relaxing. Predicted mood ninety days from now: nine out of ten. Permanent.

[dry] What actually happens is well-studied. You accept the offer. Day three, you're texting seven people. Your dopamine has, briefly, the same chemistry as a small lottery win. Real mood: eight out of ten. [pause]

[matter-of-fact] Day twenty-one, you stop noticing the salary. You start noticing your manager's tone in standups. Mood: seven point four.

Day sixty, you learn what your peer at the next company makes. You feel poorer than you did three months ago, on a higher salary. Mood: six point nine.

Day ninety, you're at baseline. Same mood as before, slightly more tax, more meetings. The thing you wanted did exactly what the data said it would do.

[wry] That's miswanting. Note carefully what *did* happen — the salary went up, the calmer apartment was real, the meetings were real. What *did not* happen is the thing your brain promised you: the permanent shift in how you feel.

[switching to Hinglish, warm] Bhai, scene yeh hai. Tu Mahindra Thar le aaya. Pehle chaudah din — society mein hero. Caretaker bhi salute marta hai. Day thirty: gaadi. Day sixty: parking ka issue. [chuckles] Day ninety — yaar Defender lena chahiye tha.

[returning to English, dry] The Thar didn't change. You adapted. And now you want a Defender — same loop, bigger EMI.

[firm, slowly] So the diagnostic question — and you should write this one down — is this. *When I imagine getting this thing, am I imagining the peak of getting it, or am I imagining a regular Tuesday afternoon, ninety days after?*

[matter-of-fact] If your imagination is doing the peak, that's the photographer talking. The Tuesday afternoon is the real life on the other side. [pause] Most people never imagine the Tuesday.

### Speech block 4 — *Move 2: the arrival fallacy*  *(target 6:50 – 9:40)*

[curious] The second move is called the *arrival fallacy.* It's miswanting with a finish line.

[slowly] *"Once I crack the JEE, life starts."* *"Once I hit FIRE, I will relax."* *"Once the wedding is over, we will breathe."* [pause]

[matter-of-fact] Tal Ben-Shahar, a professor at Harvard who taught the most-popular course in the university's history, named this one. He named it after winning his own squash championship. [wry] He won, expected ecstasy, got about eleven minutes of it, and then went looking for the next finish line.

[firm] His big insight is this. The brain treats arrival as a *transition*, not a *destination.* And the second your foot crosses the line, the brain quietly installs the next one. Not in a year. Not in a month. [emphatic] Within hours.

[thoughtful] Picture a staircase. Each step has a flag at the top labelled *finish line*. As you reach the flag, the next one materializes higher up. The staircase is infinite, by design.

[dry] You don't have one arrival fallacy. You have a *queue* of them. Promotion. Then house. Then car. Then second house. Then kid's school admission. Then their JEE. Then their wedding. Then their kid's school. Each arrival quietly hands the baton to the next, and you mistake the running for progress.

[gentle] The fix is not to stop wanting. Wanting, again, is fine. The fix is to ask the diagnostic question we just installed. *What does my Tuesday afternoon look like ninety days after I cross this line?* [pause] If the answer is "basically the same as today," you have caught a miswant in the wild.

[matter-of-fact] Two clarifications worth saying clearly. First, this does not mean every want is a miswant. Some changes really do shift your baseline. We'll cover those in lessons two and four. Second — and this is the uncomfortable one — the *bigger* the change, the louder the photographer, the worse your forecast tends to be. Promotions, weddings, and houses are *more* miswant-prone than smaller things, not less.

[switching to Hinglish, warm] Twelfth boards: "Beta, IIT crack ho jaaye, set hai." [pause]
IIT crack: "Beta, ab MNC mein placement, set hai."
MNC placement: "Beta, ab US ka onsite, set hai."
US onsite: "Beta, ab shaadi, set hai." [chuckles]
Shaadi: "Beta, ab bachcha, set hai."
Bachcha: "Beta, uski twelfth boards, set hai."

[returning to English, dry] The loop is yours. The finish lines are yours. The fatigue is yours. Aunty is just the commentator.

### Speech block 5 — *Try it and reflect*  *(target 9:40 – 12:00)*

[warm] Time to make this real. Not for Aarav, not for the Thar in the previous example. For you.

[firm] Here is the exercise. List three things you currently believe will make you noticeably happier. They can be small. They can be big. They should be specific. [pause] For each one, write two pieces of information.

[matter-of-fact] First. The realistic duration of the bump. If it's a phone, that's about fourteen days. If it's a job, eight to twelve weeks. If it's an international trip, two to four weeks of residual glow. Don't make these up; ground them in the patterns we just covered.

Second. What your day-to-day will look like ninety days after. [emphatic] Not the highlight reel. Not the photo on Instagram. The Tuesday afternoon. The grocery run. The unloading. The recurring negotiation with whoever you live with. The weather.

[thoughtful] If you can't picture the Tuesday afternoon, that's information. It probably means you've been imagining the peak, and the post-arrival Tuesday is sitting in your brain's wastebasket.

[brisk] Quick recap. Your brain is a wedding photographer with a broken memory card; it saves only the peak and the end. Miswanting is the prediction error that follows. The arrival fallacy is miswanting with a finish line, and finish lines come in queues, not singles. External bumps fade in two to twelve weeks; nothing in the dataset disagrees. The diagnostic question is the Tuesday afternoon, ninety days from now.

[firm] Five things to take with you. [pause] One. Your brain saves only two frames per event. Two. Miswanting is a prediction error, not a desire problem. Three. The arrival fallacy installs a new finish line the moment you cross the old one. Four. External bumps fade in two to twelve weeks, reliably. Five. The diagnostic question is the Tuesday afternoon, ninety days from now.

[gentle] And the reflection prompt. Write down one finish line you are currently running toward. What is the real, hour-by-hour life you expect on the day after you cross it? [pause] Take your time on that one.

[warm] We'll see you in lesson two. Where we explain why even the things that *do* feel good, stop feeling good. [wry] The thermostat is coming.
