### Speech block 1 — *Opening: the raise that disappeared*  *(target 0:00 – 1:30)*

[Indian-English accent, calm Bengaluru lecturer tone] [matter-of-fact] Welcome to Money Fundamentals. Five lessons. One promise — by the end, you will have *fewer* anxieties about money, not more. Most personal-finance content adds new things to worry about. This course removes them.

[wry] Let us begin with a small story. Aarav, twenty-eight, product manager in Whitefield. Last March his salary went up by thirty percent. ₹15 lakh per annum became ₹19 and a half. [pause] Aarav was excited. Aarav posted on Instagram. Aarav booked a Goa trip.

[dry] In November — eight months later — Aarav messaged his college friend at eleven p.m. *Bhai, my SIP is still ₹10K. Where did the raise go?* [pause]

[matter-of-fact] The raise went exactly where every raise goes by default. Into a bigger flat. A new phone. Two new subscriptions. Cab instead of metro. Slightly nicer restaurants. None of these decisions were *bad*. None of them were even *visible* as decisions. They just happened.

[firm] That is lifestyle inflation. The single biggest reason most Indian professionals look up at thirty-five and realise they have a high salary, a high lifestyle, and a low net worth.

[curious] We will name it precisely, measure it, and install one rule that catches ninety percent of it. Two moves. One rule. Ten minutes a month, forever.

### Speech block 2 — *Mental model: income vs spending vs net worth*  *(target 1:30 – 4:00)*

[firm] Let us install the mental model first, because the model is the entire course in one slide.

[slowly] Most people believe — *more income equals more wealth.* [pause] That equation is wrong. It is the most consequentially wrong equation in personal finance.

[matter-of-fact] The correct equation. *Wealth equals the gap between income and spending, compounded over time.* Three nouns. Income. Spending. Gap. Wealth is the gap, not the income.

[dry] Income is what comes in. Spending is what leaves. Gap is what stays. Wealth is what the gap becomes after a few decades of compounding. [pause] If income goes up by thirty percent and spending goes up by thirty percent, the gap is *unchanged*. Your retirement age is *unchanged*. The raise was, in net-worth terms, decorative.

[wry] Aarav's thirty-percent raise produced zero rupees of additional wealth, because his spending climbed in lockstep. He did not get a raise. He got an inflation-matched cost-of-living adjustment that he financed himself.

[curious] Now — is the answer to never spend, never enjoy, live on rice and dal in a hostel? [scoffs lightly] No. That is the other failure mode and we will cover it. The answer is to *control the ratio.* Specifically — when income goes up by X, spending should go up by significantly less than X, and the gap should expand.

[switching to Hinglish, warm] Mummy ka rule yaad hai? *"Pehle se zyada kamane lage ho, toh pehle se zyada bachao bhi.* Salary badhi hai, expense thoda hi badhao, baaki bachao." [chuckles] Mummy ne CFA nahi kiya. Mummy ne Excel nahi banaya. Mummy ne sirf yeh ek line bola, har raise ke baad, har saal.

[returning to English, gentle] She was running the entire model. Income went up; spending went up by less; the gap expanded; the gap got invested in jewellery, FDs, the house. By sixty her household had real net worth. The neighbour with the bigger car did not.

[firm] So. Income, spending, gap, wealth. Memorize the four words. Every other lesson in this course is a more specific application of this one model.

### Speech block 3 — *Move 1: lifestyle inflation, named and measured*  *(target 4:00 – 7:20)*

[firm] Move one. Catch lifestyle inflation in the act.

[matter-of-fact] You cannot fix what you cannot see, and lifestyle inflation is almost invisible by design. It happens in small steps — a slightly bigger flat at lease renewal; a slightly nicer phone at upgrade time; a slightly more expensive dinner spot becomes the default. None of these announce themselves.

[curious] Here is the diagnostic. Whenever your monthly income goes up — raise, bonus, side gig — write down the new number, *and* write down what your savings rate was the month before. Then watch the savings rate three months later. If it is the same or lower, lifestyle inflation has eaten the raise.

[dry] Most people do not run this diagnostic because the answer is uncomfortable. The diagnostic takes ninety seconds. The discomfort lasts longer.

[matter-of-fact] Run a real example. Salary goes from ₹1.25 lakh per month to ₹1.6 lakh per month — a ₹35K bump. Three months later, your monthly SIP is unchanged at ₹15K. Your bank balance growth is unchanged. The ₹35K is gone. [pause] It went somewhere. The default destinations are well documented — bigger flat, nicer phone, more cabs, more food delivery, one new subscription.

[switching to Hinglish, warm] Bro from college LinkedIn pe poha pe puchta hai — *"Yaar, Mahindra Thar le rahe ho ab? Bonus aaya na?"* Tu galat sun leta hai. Bro literally suggest kar raha hai *"apna paisa khareed ke phenk de."* [chuckles]

[returning to English, dry] The Mahindra-Thar-by-thirty trap. Salary plus thirty K per month. Thar EMI — thirty-five K per month. Net cash flow — *minus* five K per month. Five years later — full Thar paid off. Bro from college now has the Defender. You sell the Thar at sixty percent of on-road price. [pause] Five years of EMIs equals roughly twenty-one lakh. The same twenty-one lakh in a Nifty-50 index for five years compounds to about thirty-five lakh. The car did not cost twenty-one lakh. The car cost thirty-five lakh in *forgone* wealth, plus depreciation, plus the SIP that never happened.

[dry] Five years of car EMIs at ₹35K a month equals ₹21 lakh. The same ₹21 lakh in a Nifty index over the same period would have grown to ₹35 lakh. The car didn't cost ₹21 lakh; it cost ₹35 lakh in forgone wealth. Lifestyle inflation has a brand name.
-->

[firm] One specific antidote. The day a raise lands in your account, *automate* the increase to your SIP *the same day.* Not next month. Not after Diwali. Not after the wedding. Same day. The brain has not yet inflated to match the new number; you can capture the gap before lifestyle does.

[wry] If you wait two months, the brain has already mentally allocated the extra. Trying to claw it back feels like a pay cut. Capturing it on day one feels like nothing changed. Same money. Different timing. Wildly different psychology.

### Speech block 4 — *Move 2: the 50-30-20 rule (and when to break it)*  *(target 7:20 – 10:00)*

[firm] Move two. The 50-30-20 rule.

[matter-of-fact] After-tax income, split three ways. *Fifty percent for needs.* Rent, groceries, EMIs you cannot avoid, utilities, insurance premiums, basic transport. *Thirty percent for wants.* Eating out, travel, gadgets, subscriptions, that fancy gym membership. *Twenty percent for savings and investments.* SIPs, emergency fund, retirement.

[dry] Fifty, thirty, twenty. Not because it is mathematically optimal. Because it is *memorable*, and a rule you actually remember beats a spreadsheet you never open.

[curious] Now the honest part. The fifty-thirty-twenty rule was designed for a household where housing costs about thirty percent of income. In Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi NCR — that assumption breaks. Rent for a one-BHK in HSR Layout is forty percent of a ₹1.5 lakh salary by itself. The fifty-percent needs bucket gets blown immediately.

[matter-of-fact] So break the rule, but break it deliberately. The non-negotiable is *the twenty percent savings line*. Defend that line first. Then squeeze needs and wants to fit whatever's left. If you make ₹1.5 lakh per month, ₹30,000 goes to savings before any other decision. The remaining ₹1.2 lakh splits across rent, food, fun.

[firm, slowly] Pay yourself first. Live on what is left. Not the other way around.

[switching to Hinglish, warm] Mummy ka kitchen rule remember? Pehle ration ka paisa side mein, fir baaki ka kharcha. Ration ka envelope kabhi touch nahi hota tha. *Pehle bachao, fir kharcha karo.* Same logic. Same envelope. Just digital ab. [chuckles]

[returning to English, dry] The reason this works is automation, not willpower. Schedule the SIP for the second of every month — the day after salary lands. The money is gone before your brain decides what to do with it. You spend whatever is left in the account, because that is all there is. The discipline is in the schedule. Not in your head.

[matter-of-fact] One trap to flag. The bonus. Diwali bonus, performance bonus, Pongal bonus — does not matter. Mental accounting tells you bonuses are *different* from salary. They are not. Money is fungible. The same ₹2 lakh that would have been disciplined as salary becomes a Goa trip as a bonus. [pause] Pre-commit. Decide *before* the bonus lands what percentage goes to savings. Fifty-thirty-twenty works fine for bonuses too. The decision is the system; the spending follows automatically.

[wry] EMIs deserve a special call-out. EMI is lifestyle inflation, on contract. The bank shows you the monthly number — *only ₹35K per month for five years* — and hides the multiplied number — *₹21 lakh over five years*. Always multiply the EMI by the term before you sign anything. Always. Twenty seconds of arithmetic prevents twenty years of regret.

### Speech block 5 — *Try it and reflect*  *(target 10:00 – 12:00)*

[warm] Time to make this real.

[firm] Open your last three months of salary credits. Right now. Phone. Banking app. The number that landed in your account — write it down. Three numbers, three months.

[matter-of-fact] Now write down your SIP amount across those same three months. If your salary went up at any point in those three months and your SIP did not — that is your lifestyle inflation, named, dated, sized.

[wry, slowly] If the answer is *I don't know my SIP amount off the top of my head*, that is also information. It means the most important number in your financial life is something you have not looked at in months. We can fix that in one sitting.

[firm] One concrete action before this lesson ends. Increase your SIP by five percent of your last raise — even if the raise was six months ago. Five percent is small enough that you will not feel it, large enough that it will compound to several lakh over a working career. The increase is the action. The amount is almost beside the point.

[brisk] Recap. Move one — catch lifestyle inflation by tracking your savings rate after every income change. Move two — fifty-thirty-twenty as a default; defend the twenty before anything else.

[firm] Five takeaways. [pause] One. Wealth is the *gap* between income and spending, compounded — not the income itself. Two. Lifestyle inflation eats raises silently because it happens in small unconscious steps. Three. Automate the SIP increase the same day a raise lands; before the brain inflates to match. Four. Fifty-thirty-twenty is a memorable default; the only non-negotiable is the twenty percent savings line. Five. EMIs and bonuses both lie about size; multiply EMIs by the term, pre-commit a percentage of bonuses.

[gentle] The reflection prompt for this lesson. *What is your savings rate this month, exactly? And what was it the month before your last raise?*

[warm] Lesson two is about the single most important pre-condition for any of this to work — the boring buffer that fixes downstream risk-taking. We will install your emergency fund. [wry] You will be tempted to skip it. Do not skip it. Without it, every other move in this course collapses on the first bad week.
