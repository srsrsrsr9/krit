Chapter 01 · Start
Calibration set. Now the actual page begins.

The wall keyword search hit

Tuesday, 11:40 AM. Your PM Slacks: "Search returns nothing for 'kid-friendly restaurant'. Fix it."

You check the query. Postgres LIKE '%kid-friendly%'. Users mean family-friendly, no-alcohol, good for kids. None of those phrases sit verbatim in your data, so the query returns the empty set. Adding synonyms helps until a content team in another city is maintaining 3,400 entries and Diwali traffic still drops 8% because someone forgot to add phirni.

This is the wall keyword search hits. Crossing it — turning text into numbers in a way that lets meaning be compared — is the move that everything we now call "AI" is built on. That move is the embedding.

The interesting word in "high-dimensional pattern matching" isn't pattern. It's high-dimensional. The dimensions are the model.

Words as numbers — the intuition

Pick any word. Imagine plotting it on a graph. With one axis — say, "size" — you can place elephant far from mouse. With two axes — "size" and "tameness" — you can separate elephant, mouse, and lion. With three axes you start being able to disambiguate bank (river) from bank (money): add an axis for "is about money?".

Now imagine 384 axes. Or 1,536. Each one a learned semantic dimension you didn't have to name — the model figured out which axes carry useful signal during training.

That's an embedding. A vector of (e.g.) 384 floats, one per axis, where similar meanings produce nearby vectors. The unit of comparison is cosine similarity: a number from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (identical).

Cheat sheet
  • 1.00 — same vector (the word with itself, or its exact duplicate)
  • 0.75–0.95 — strong semantic relationship ("king" vs "queen")
  • 0.50–0.75 — related but distinct ("king" vs "throne")
  • 0.20–0.50 — vaguely related ("king" vs "country")
  • 0.00–0.20 — unrelated ("king" vs "pencil")
Rough ranges for sentence-transformer embeddings on English. Different models give slightly different absolute values; what matters is the relative ordering.

Chapter 02 · Why
You can name the wall, and you've committed your first prediction.

How embeddings actually get built (the 90-second version)

The original trick — Word2Vec, 2013 — was deceptively simple. Take a sentence. Predict each word from its neighbours. The model has a 300-dimensional hidden layer; force it to learn good representations to win the prediction game.

The breakthrough was that the side effect of the prediction task was a meaning space. After training, words that play similar roles in sentences ended up with similar vectors. "king" – "man" + "woman" ≈ "queen" wasn't a feature anyone designed. It fell out of forcing the model to predict context.

Modern embeddings use the same idea with three upgrades: (1) the model is a transformer encoder (so context matters — bank in a river sentence vs a finance sentence get different vectors), (2) the prediction task is richer (masked language modelling, contrastive learning), (3) the output is per-sentence, not per-word.

For 90% of production use, you don't need to know any of this. You pick a model (bge-small-en-v1.5, all-MiniLM-L6-v2), call .encode(), and the floats come out. The intuition above matters for debugging: when embeddings give weird results, it's usually because the model was trained on data unlike yours, not because the math is wrong.

Open the black box — build one in 60 lines

You've used a pretrained embedding model. Now we'll build a tiny one from scratch — small enough that nothing is hiding. Three short walkthroughs in PyTorch, then we'll read the whole file as one piece. By the end, the line "embeddings are a lookup table that training reshapes" will stop being a metaphor.

First walkthrough — what's in the table and what feeds it. Second — the math that reshapes it. Third — the loop that runs the math a thousand times. A scrubbable, Karpathy-style read of the whole file follows.

Walkthrough 1 · The data and the model

# PyTorch only — no other libraries.
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

Standard imports. nn.Embedding is the lookup table; F.cosine_similarity is the comparison we'll train against. Three lines, no surprises. 

# Toy vocabulary. Real models train on 30k+ tokens; we use 10.
vocab = ['cat',   'dog',  'puppy', 'kitten', 'king',
         'queen', 'man',  'woman', 'apple',  'banana']
w2i = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
# w2i['cat']    == 0
# w2i['banana'] == 9

Embedding models work on integer indices, not strings. The w2i map is what tokenisation does in real systems — convert text into a sequence of integers the model can look up. Everything downstream operates on integers. 

# The training signal. The ONLY place we tell the model what 'meaning' is.
similar = [('cat',   'kitten'), ('dog',   'puppy'),
           ('king',  'queen'),  ('apple', 'banana')]

dissimilar = [('cat',    'king'),   ('apple', 'queen'),
              ('puppy',  'banana'), ('kitten','man')]

Eight pairs. Four to pull together, four to push apart. The model has no other ground truth — no dictionary, no part-of-speech tags, no concept of "animal" or "royalty." It only sees: these two indices should end up close; these two should end up far.

Real models get this signal at scale by treating co-occurrence as a proxy: words that appear in the same sentence are "similar," words from random sentences are "dissimilar." Same mechanism, billions of pairs. 

# The model. Literally a lookup table: 10 words × 3 dimensions = 30 numbers.
EMB_DIM = 3
embeddings = nn.Embedding(num_embeddings=len(vocab), embedding_dim=EMB_DIM)

That's the entire model. Not a metaphor — nn.Embedding(10, 3) allocates a 10×3 matrix of random floats and exposes a lookup. Index 0 returns row 0. Index 9 returns row 9. The matrix is what we train.

Production embedding models stack a transformer encoder on top of this lookup so context can modulate each vector. The foundation — the lookup — is identical. 

# "Embedding a word" = looking up its row.
cat_vec = embeddings(torch.tensor(w2i['cat']))
print(cat_vec)
# tensor([-0.31, 1.42, 0.07], grad_fn=<EmbeddingBackward>)

Three numbers. Right now they mean nothing — the matrix was filled with random floats when we created it. Training will reshape those numbers so 'cat' and 'kitten' end up close and 'cat' and 'king' end up far.

Walkthrough 2 · The math that does the nudging

Walkthrough 1 left us with a randomly-filled table and a list of pairs. This walkthrough is where the math turns into code. Two questions: what's a "loss" and what does training actually do to the table? 

# Cosine similarity in two lines. Dot product divided by magnitudes.
def cosine(a, b):
    return (a * b).sum() / (a.norm() * b.norm())

That's the formula. F.cosine_similarity does the same thing with batch handling; we wrote it by hand so nothing is hidden. Output: a scalar between −1 (opposite directions) and 1 (same direction). 

# The loss: a single number. How wrong are we, right now?
def contrastive_loss(emb, similar, dissimilar):
    pull = 0.0
    for w1, w2 in similar:
        v1 = emb(torch.tensor(w2i[w1]))
        v2 = emb(torch.tensor(w2i[w2]))
        pull += (1 - cosine(v1, v2))             # 0 when identical, grows as they drift apart

    push = 0.0
    for w1, w2 in dissimilar:
        v1 = emb(torch.tensor(w2i[w1]))
        v2 = emb(torch.tensor(w2i[w2]))
        push += torch.clamp(cosine(v1, v2) + 0.3, min=0)  # fires only if too close

    return (pull + push) / (len(similar) + len(dissimilar))

Read it in two halves. The pull half loops over similar pairs and adds (1 − cosine) — high when the pair is far apart, zero when they're identical. The push half loops over dissimilar pairs and adds the cosine if it's still above −0.3 — i.e. it only fires when they're not yet pushed apart enough.

Loss = how much we're wrong. Lower is better. Right now (random table), the loss is around 0.9. After training it'll be near 0.05. 

# An optimizer. Plain SGD with a learning rate.
opt = torch.optim.SGD(embeddings.parameters(), lr=0.5)

# ── ONE training step ──
opt.zero_grad()                                # forget last step's gradient
loss = contrastive_loss(embeddings, similar, dissimilar)
loss.backward()                                # gradient of loss w.r.t. every embedding
opt.step()                                     # nudge each vector against its gradient

Four lines that do the whole thing. .backward() is where PyTorch shines: for every one of the 30 numbers in the table, it computes the gradient — which direction would lower the loss. opt.step() nudges each number a little in that direction. One pair at a time, the table reshapes.

Walkthrough 3 · The loop, the query, the result

One step nudges a little. Two hundred steps teach the table. After that, "encode a word" returns a vector that actually means something — meaning being whatever the pairs told it. 

# Training loop. 200 steps.
for step in range(200):
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = contrastive_loss(embeddings, similar, dissimilar)
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
    if step % 40 == 0:
        cs = cosine(embeddings(torch.tensor(w2i['cat'])),
                    embeddings(torch.tensor(w2i['kitten']))).item()
        print(f'step {step:3d}  loss {loss.item():.3f}  cos(cat, kitten) {cs:.3f}')

# step   0  loss 0.873  cos(cat, kitten) 0.041
# step  40  loss 0.231  cos(cat, kitten) 0.751
# step  80  loss 0.124  cos(cat, kitten) 0.891
# step 120  loss 0.087  cos(cat, kitten) 0.933
# step 160  loss 0.063  cos(cat, kitten) 0.957

The whole table reshapes. cos(cat, kitten) climbs from random (0.04) toward near-synonymous (0.96). The other pairs reshape in parallel. We never told the model what a cat is — only that 'cat' should end up near 'kitten'. The semantic structure is a side effect of the pulls and pushes. 

# Query: encode a word, find the nearest neighbours in the vocab.
query = embeddings(torch.tensor(w2i['queen']))
results = []
for w, i in w2i.items():
    if w == 'queen': continue
    sim = cosine(query, embeddings(torch.tensor(i))).item()
    results.append((w, sim))
results.sort(key=lambda x: -x[1])
print(results[:3])
# [('king', 0.94), ('woman', 0.62), ('man', 0.31)]

    Section 1 / 5 · Imports
    # A 60-line, single-file contrastive embedding trainer.
# Run: python embed_from_scratch.py
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

# ── DATA ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
vocab = ['cat',   'dog',  'puppy', 'kitten', 'king',
         'queen', 'man',  'woman', 'apple',  'banana']
w2i = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}

similar    = [('cat',   'kitten'), ('dog',   'puppy'),
              ('king',  'queen'),  ('apple', 'banana')]
dissimilar = [('cat',    'king'),   ('apple', 'queen'),
              ('puppy',  'banana'), ('kitten','man')]

# ── MODEL ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EMB_DIM = 3
embeddings = nn.Embedding(len(vocab), EMB_DIM)

def cosine(a, b):
    return (a * b).sum() / (a.norm() * b.norm())

# ── LOSS + OPTIMIZER ─────────────────────────────────────────
def contrastive_loss(emb, similar, dissimilar):
    pull = sum((1 - cosine(emb(torch.tensor(w2i[a])),
                           emb(torch.tensor(w2i[b]))))
               for a, b in similar)
    push = sum(torch.clamp(cosine(emb(torch.tensor(w2i[a])),
                                  emb(torch.tensor(w2i[b]))) + 0.3, min=0)
               for a, b in dissimilar)
    return (pull + push) / (len(similar) + len(dissimilar))

opt = torch.optim.SGD(embeddings.parameters(), lr=0.5)

# ── TRAIN ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
for step in range(200):
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = contrastive_loss(embeddings, similar, dissimilar)
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()

# ── QUERY ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
query = embeddings(torch.tensor(w2i['queen']))
results = []
for w, i in w2i.items():
    if w == 'queen': continue
    sim = cosine(query, embeddings(torch.tensor(i))).item()
    results.append((w, sim))
results.sort(key=lambda x: -x[1])
print(results[:3])
# [('king', 0.94), ('woman', 0.62), ('man', 0.31)]
    Imports. Just PyTorch. Three lines, no surprises. Everything else is built from these.

    That's the whole thing. Sixty lines of Python that does, at toy scale, what a 384-dimensional production embedding model does at industrial scale. The only thing missing for production is the data, the depth, and the optimizer hyperparameters. The shape is identical.

    What this one primitive unlocks

    You've now built (in your head and in your terminal) the single thing modern AI is mostly made of. Here's what it's used for in production, with one-line "why this works":

    Semantic search

    Encode every document; encode the query; return the top-K by cosine. Why it works: the embedding captures meaning, not surface form. Synonyms, paraphrases, related concepts all rank well without manual synonym lists.

    Classification

    Encode each labelled example. At inference, encode the new item, find the K nearest labelled examples, vote. Why it works: embeddings cluster by meaning, so K-NN finds genuine semantic neighbours. Often beats fine-tuned classifiers on small datasets.

    RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

    The R is embeddings. You chunk your docs, embed each chunk, and at query time retrieve the top-K relevant chunks to stuff into an LLM prompt. Why it works: grounds the LLM in your specific data without finetuning. The LLM never had to memorise your docs; it just reads them at query time.

    Clustering / deduplication

    Embed everything, run K-means or HDBSCAN. Items with high cosine similarity merge. Why it works: the embedding space is a real metric space — distances are meaningful, so traditional unsupervised methods just work.

    Recommendations

    Embed users by what they engaged with; embed items; return nearest items. Why it works: behavioural and content signals project into the same space, so the comparison is principled.

    Three pitfalls (the things people get wrong)

    1. Picking the wrong model for your domain

    Models like all-MiniLM-L6-v2 are trained on general English. If your data is heavily Indian context (Hinglish reviews, Indian regulatory docs), use bge-small-en-v1.5 or test multilingual models like paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2. The cheapest experiment: encode 20 representative items, eyeball the similarity matrix. If pairs you'd expect to match score below 0.4, the model is wrong for your data.

    2. Confusing cosine, dot product, and Euclidean

    Cosine measures the angle between vectors (direction only — magnitudes ignored). Dot product factors in magnitude (so longer vectors score higher). Euclidean measures the straight-line distance. For most sentence-transformer models, the vectors come pre-normalised, so cosine and dot product give the same answer. When in doubt, use cosine.

    3. Forgetting that embeddings drift

    If you upgrade your embedding model, every cached vector in your DB becomes stale. They were computed by a different model. You either re-embed everything (one-time cost) or you live with two parallel index versions. Architect for this from day one — store the model name alongside each vector in your DB.
