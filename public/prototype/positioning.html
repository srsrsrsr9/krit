Krit Deep Dives · Marketing Issue 01 · Positioning
A short essay, three cases, one diagnostic

Same drink,
different question.

Two bottled waters. Same H2O molecule, same factory equipment, same regulatory body, same shelf. One sells for ₹20 at any kirana in Bangalore. The other sells for the rupee equivalent of ₹165 at the Whole Foods near Indiranagar — six times the price for the same molecule. Both are profitable. Both are growing.

The difference between them is the question they answered. This essay is about that question, and why most brands never figure out which one they're answering.

Case 01

The water that sounds like a heavy metal band.

In 2017, a former Netflix executive launched a beverage company. The product was water in an aluminium can. The brand was called Liquid Death. The cans had skulls on them and read "murder your thirst." By 2022 the company raised at a $1.4B post-money valuation; by 2024 it was on every Whole Foods shelf in the US and on the better-curated Indian boutique-grocery shelves. Meanwhile, Bisleri — the country's largest bottled water brand, founded in 1965 — was reporting flat growth and competing on price with private labels.

Both brands sell water. So why is one of them treated like a tech startup and the other treated like a commodity? Look at them side by side. Don't think about pricing yet — think about who would buy this and what they'd compare it to.

Liquid Death
"Murder Your Thirst." Tallboy aluminum cans. Heavy metal aesthetic. Tagline: "Death to Plastic."
₹165 / can · sold in nightclubs, skate shops, Whole Foods
Bisleri
"Drink it. Trust it." 500ml plastic bottle. Family-safe blue label. Marketing message: "Pure mineral water for healthy living."
₹20 / bottle · sold in kirana stores, train stations, restaurants

Click each question to see how a professional marketer would answer it for Liquid Death.

slot 1Who is this for?
The 22–35-year-old who orders craft beer at the bar but is doing a sober October. The "I don't want to be the only one not drinking" demographic. People who care about how their hand looks holding a beverage. Not really thirsty people — identity-conscious socializers.
slot 2Against what alternative?
Not other waters. Soda and beer. Liquid Death is positioned against your other can-in-hand options at a bar or party. That's why it's in a tallboy can, that's why it's called what it's called. The implicit message: "drinking water at this bar wouldn't be embarrassing if your water looked like THIS."
slot 3Why this and not the alternative?
Because the social cost of holding it is zero — and possibly negative (i.e., it's cool). Beer = calories, hangover, and now increasingly uncool among health-conscious millennials. Soda = sugar, kid energy. Liquid Death = looks like the rebellious thing, costs you nothing the next morning. The product is hydration, but the job is "permit me to not drink without looking like I'm not drinking."

Now do the same exercise for Bisleri. The slot 1 answer is "Indian families who care about not getting waterborne illness." Slot 2: "tap water, unbranded bottled water." Slot 3: "safer than tap, brand trust built over 60 years."

Notice the gap. Bisleri's three answers are good and complete — they're not bad positioning. But the positioning is for a 1965 problem: "is this water safe?" Liquid Death's positioning is for a 2024 problem: "what does my hand look like at the bar?" Different question, different price.

The frame, named

Three slots. Fill all three or you don't have positioning — you have a product description.

Slot 1
Audience

Who specifically. Not "everyone who drinks water." Specific enough that you'd recognise this person walking down the street.

Slot 2
Alternative

What they'd buy if you didn't exist. Often not a direct competitor. Liquid Death's alternative is beer, not Bisleri.

Slot 3
Angle

The one reason this beats that alternative for that audience. Not three reasons. Not "high quality." One sharp claim.

Case 02

Two work-chat apps. Two different ceilings.

In 2013, Slack launched. Its founders said publicly that they weren't trying to beat existing chat tools — they were trying to kill email. In 2017, Microsoft Teams launched. Its launch positioning, repeated in keynotes and marketing material, was simpler: "Like Slack, but free if you have Office 365."

Both companies grew. But the shape of their growth was completely different. Slack ran away with the developer and early-stage market and was acquired by Salesforce in late 2020 for $27.7B — a strong outcome, and one that ratified a ceiling: a top-down purchase by a giant whose existing buyers were asking the IT-procurement question Teams was built to answer. Teams meanwhile quietly reached 320 million daily active users by 2024 — the largest collaboration tool on Earth, by a factor of five.

Before reading further: which positioning do you think aged better? Slack's "kill email" or Teams' "like Slack but free"?

Slack
"Where work happens." Positioned against the email inbox. Slogan-era tagline: "Be less busy." Targeted modern teams who felt email was broken.
Microsoft Teams
"The hub for teamwork." Positioned against Slack. Marketing message: "All the chat. None of the bill." Targeted IT departments at Office 365 customers.
verdictWhose positioning aged better, and why?
Slack's positioning was better but more fragile. Teams' was worse but more durable.

Slack picked a giant alternative (email) which gave them runway to grow into for years — most knowledge workers really did hate email. But "kill email" doesn't tell IT departments what to do at procurement time. So Slack thrived bottom-up (engineers installed it) and stalled at the buying-committee level.

Teams picked a smaller alternative (Slack itself) but gave IT departments the one thing they needed: "it's already paid for." That positioning didn't sound exciting, but it answered the question that was actually being asked in 2017–2022 budget reviews. Teams' positioning was boring AND correct.

The general lesson: a positioning that sounds visionary often loses to a positioning that answers the question your buyer is asking right now.
"Mediocre answers to live questions beat brilliant answers to abstract ones." — The whole point of Case 02
Case 03

Notion: when positioning eliminates the question.

The strongest version of positioning isn't picking a side in a fight. It's making the fight feel obsolete. Notion's pitch, when it launched in 2016, did this — and it's worth dissecting because the move is harder than it looks.

Three statements about Notion are printed below. Each one fills a different slot — Audience, Alternative, or Angle. Drag the dropdowns to match each statement to its slot. Get all three right and you'll see the deeper move.

"Most workspaces force you to pick: Word for documents, Excel for spreadsheets, Trello for tasks, Confluence for wiki — and then nothing fits together."
"Small teams (5–50 people) who care more about clarity than process — designers, startup founders, solo creators, product teams."
"One tool where docs, tables, tasks, and pages all live in the same canvas — so you stop choosing and start writing."

Notice what Notion's "alternative" slot does. It's not Word or Excel or Confluence — those are the incumbents. The alternative Notion picked was "the act of having to choose between them at all." That's a sneakier and more powerful move: you reframe the buyer's problem so that the obvious incumbents become symptoms of a deeper inefficiency. Once the buyer accepts the reframe, none of the incumbents can answer them.

If you can change what your buyer is asking, you've already won — they'll come to you to answer.

The diagnostic

Spot the broken slot.

Four brand statements below. Each one looks plausible on first read. Each one has exactly one slot that's missing or vague. Identify it. Audience answers tend to fail by being "everyone." Alternative answers tend to fail by being generic ("the old way"). Angle answers tend to fail by being unfalsifiable ("better quality").

"A modern productivity platform that helps anyone do their best work, replacing inefficient tools with one unified workspace built for the future of teams."
Audience. "Anyone" is the giveaway. Combined with "future of teams" and "best work," there's no recognisable person here. You can't picture them, you can't pitch them. Compare to "small teams, 5–50 people, who care more about clarity than process."
"A new approach to financial planning for young professionals, helping them grow their wealth more effectively than traditional methods."
Alternative. "Traditional methods" is the tell. What specifically — a financial advisor? A bank RM? A robo-advisor? Just doing nothing? Each implies a different product and a different price point. The vague alternative makes the angle ("more effective") un-checkable too. Specificity in slot 2 forces honesty in slot 3.
"A high-quality coffee subscription for serious coffee drinkers in metros, delivered to your door."
Angle. "High-quality" tells you nothing falsifiable. Every coffee brand calls itself high-quality. The angle should answer: why would a serious coffee drinker who currently has a favourite local roastery switch to a subscription? Convenience? Variety? Education? Pick one and commit. "High-quality" is the angle equivalent of saying "we're good."
"A new kind of educational platform that uses AI to make every learner's experience personalised, replacing one-size-fits-all online courses."
Audience. "Every learner" — children, adults, professionals, university students, hobbyists all learn under wildly different constraints. The personalisation claim isn't false, but without an audience the claim can't be designed for or marketed to. Pick a specific learner type and the rest sharpens immediately. (Notice this is the same failure mode as Q1 — most generic positioning collapses at the Audience slot first.)
Build it for yourself

Write positioning for something you're working on.

Fill the three slots below for a real project — your startup, your side project, your team's product, your own consulting offer. Write the way you'd talk to one person, not the way you'd write a press release.

The page will flag vagueness markers in your text — words like "everyone", "best", "world-class", "innovative", "next-generation". They're not banned, just usually a sign the slot is hiding something. Heuristic-driven feedback, not a judgment.

Slot 1 · Audience
Who is this for? Specific enough that you could describe what they look like, what they do at work, and one thing that frustrates them today.
Slot 2 · Alternative
What they'd reach for if you didn't exist. Name the product, the workflow, or the workaround. Not "the old way."
Slot 3 · Angle
The one reason this beats that alternative for that audience. One sharp claim. Falsifiable. Not "higher quality."
"Positioning is a question of who you're
willing to lose." — What this whole essay was really about

The hardest part of positioning isn't the writing. It's the willingness to exclude. Every time you sharpen Slot 1, you lose buyers. Every time you specify Slot 2, you lose conversations. Every time you commit to Slot 3, you forfeit other claims you could have made.

Most brands are vague because the founders are afraid to be specific. The vagueness feels safer — but the chart is brutal. Brands with sharp positioning don't grow because they're better; they grow because the right buyer recognises themselves in the first sentence and the wrong buyer leaves immediately. Both outcomes are wins. The vague brand has to convince everyone, which means convincing no one.

If you only do one thing after this essay: open a notes app, and write down — for whatever you're working on — the name of one specific person who is your audience. Then write down the name of one specific competing tool or workflow they currently use. Then write one sentence on why you're better than that thing for that person. The positioning falls out of those three names. Everything else is grammar.

Open loop · for tomorrow

Pick a brand you use daily. Write its three slots from memory.

Could be Swiggy, ChatGPT, Bisleri, BookMyShow — anything you've spent money on this month. Sit with it for five minutes tomorrow. Then look at the brand's own positioning statement on their About page or homepage and see how close you got. Most of them will fail the three-slot test you just learned — and that's the lesson. Positioning is a discipline most marketing teams never enforce on themselves.

Come back and audit one of your own brand artifacts (deck, landing page, pitch) against the diagnostic above. Specificity is a skill that develops by reps, not by reading.
