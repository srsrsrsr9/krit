Modeling Fundamentals (and When Not to)

Simplest model first; bigger model only if it pays for itself

notes by Karthik ✏️

~ 13 min read ~

Most analyst projects do not need a model. They need a number, a clean comparison, and a memo.

Mental model: simplest model first
Hold this picture. Modeling is a staircase, and each step costs roughly 10× more time and adds roughly 10× less accuracy than the step before it. The first two steps usually pay for themselves; the rest usually do not.

Move 1 — The train / val / test split

  • Three datasets, three jobs: - **Train**: the data the model fits on. - **Validation**: the data you check after each tweak.
  • Step 1 — Compute the baseline: Always-predict-no on a churn dataset where 13% churn → 87% accuracy. That is your floor. Anything you build must beat 87%, and beat it by enough to justify the maintenance cost.
  • Step 2 — Split: train (70%) / val (15%) / test (15%): Split by user, not by row. Stratify on the outcome (so churn rate is similar in each split). Save test to a separate file you literally do not load.
  • Step 3 — Fit logistic regression on train, evaluate on val: Standard scaler + logistic regression on engineered features. Look at the val accuracy, AUC, and the top 10 coefficients. Most of the model's reasoning should be human-defensible.
  • Step 4 — Try one or two more model classes (RF, XGBoost on defaults): Compare on val. If the lift over logistic regression is < 1pp, stop — the simpler model wins on operations cost.
  • Step 5 — Check for overfitting on the validation set: Run on a small held-out validation slice that you also haven't peeked at, or use cross-validation. If val accuracy mysteriously drops, you have been tuning on a leaderboard, not learning generalisation.
  • Step 6 — Run ONCE on test, report the number, do not tune further: Open the test file, evaluate, write the number in the memo. If the number is materially worse than val, report that gap honestly — it is the most useful piece of information about how the model will behave in production.

Move 2 — The OK-line

  • Before you start modeling, define the **OK-line**: the level of performance below which the model is not worth shipping, and above which more accuracy is not worth chasing. The OK-line is set by business cost and by the cost of the next-best alternative (often: a rule of thumb, or doing nothing).

Traps to flag for the exam

The classic trap: overfitting on the validation set: Most analysts know not to peek at the test set. Almost no analyst notices when they are *overfitting on the validation set* — and that is the trap that quietly poisons production models.

Takeaways (memorize these)

Most analyst questions do not need a model; they need a clean comparison and a memo. Reach for a model only when ranking or scoring at scale is genuinely the job.
The modeling staircase is logarithmic in cost: each step costs roughly 10× more and adds roughly 10× less than the step before it.
Train, val, test — and the test set is the audit; touch it once, at the end, or you have no test set.
Overfitting on the validation set is the trap nobody warns you about; cap your tweaks at 5-10 distinct variants and stop on schedule.
Define the OK-line at the start; the question is 'have I crossed it?', not 'how high can I go?'.
