PRDs, Specs, and the Art of Prioritization

A PRD is a contract with future-you, not a novel for the engineers

notes by Meera ✏️

~ 14 min read ~

Most PRDs fail in one of two directions. Either they are 14 pages of background, user personas, motivational quotes, and pixel mockups — and nobody reads past page 3.

Mental model: a PRD is a contract with future-you
When you write a PRD, the most important reader is not the engineer, the designer, or the CEO. It is **future-you**, three weeks from now, when someone asks 'wait, was this in scope?' and you have to answer without re-litigating the whole thing.

Move 1 — RICE and value-vs-effort

  • You will have more PRDs than engineers, always. Prioritization is not a skill — it is the *job*.
  • Step 1 — Score Reach honestly: Reach is users affected per quarter, not 'every user could theoretically use this.' If only 8 percent of admins ever open the export menu, your reach for an export feature is at most 8 percent.
  • Step 2 — Score Impact on a small scale: Use 0.25 / 0.5 / 1 / 2 / 3. Not 1-10. The narrower scale prevents 'everything is a 7' inflation. Impact 3 is reserved for things that materially shift retention or revenue.
  • Step 3 — Score Confidence brutally: 100 percent only if you have direct user evidence (interviews, instrumented behavior). 80 percent if you have indirect (sales reports, support tickets). 50 percent or lower for 'the CEO has a feeling'. Most early-stage features are 50-70 percent.
  • Step 4 — Estimate Effort with engineering: Person-weeks. Take the engineer's number and multiply by 1.5 for review, QA, and the one mid-project surprise. The 1.5x is empirical, not pessimism.
  • Step 5 — Sort by score, then sanity-check: Look at the top 5. Does the order match what your judgment says? If not, your inputs are off — usually confidence. Do not tweak the inputs to match your gut; either trust the gut and override (and write down why), or trust the inputs and override the gut. Be explicit about which you did.

Move 2 — Cut, don't compromise

  • Halfway through every project, you will be asked to fit more into less. Less time, less engineers, fewer designers — same scope.

Traps to flag for the exam

The classic trap: 'just one more thing' scope creep: Mid-project, someone (often the founder, occasionally sales, sometimes you) realises the feature would be 'just so much better with X.' X is small. X seems harmless. X is how 4-week features become 9-week features.

Takeaways (memorize these)

A PRD is a contract with future-you about what 'done' means. Outcome, out-of-scope, done-means, risks — those four sections, on one page.
TBD is not a placeholder; it is a deferred bug. Replace with a decision or a tracked open question with an owner and a date.
RICE and value-vs-effort don't decide for you; they make your reasoning auditable. When scores are within 15 percent, use judgment, not a tiebreaker.
When scope must shrink, cut entire features rather than compromising on every feature. Three things shipped well beat six things shipped halfway.
Mid-sprint additions require 1:1 displacement; if nothing can be displaced, the addition does not enter the sprint.
