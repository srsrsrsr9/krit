Talking to Users Without Getting Lied To

They tell you what they want; your job is to hear what they need

notes by Vikrant ✏️

~ 13 min read ~

Users will lie to you. Not maliciously — politely, helpfully, with smiles.

Mental model: users tell you what they want, not what they need
Treat every user statement as having two channels:

Move 1 — 'Tell me about the last time…'

  • The single most useful sentence in user research is **'Tell me about the last time you…'** It does three things at once. First, it forces past-tense.
  • Step 1 — Stop asking 'would you': Strike every future-tense question from your interview script. If you have 'would you use', 'would you pay', 'how often would you', delete them. They don't survive contact with reality.
  • Step 2 — Replace with 'tell me about the last time': Convert every aspiration into a memory probe. 'Would you use an export feature?' becomes 'Tell me about the last time you needed to get data out of the dashboard.'
  • Step 3 — Follow the workaround: When they describe what they did, ask what tool they used, what was annoying, how long it took. Workarounds are revealed need with a price tag attached.
  • Step 4 — Probe for absence: If they cannot recall a single instance in the last 30-90 days, that is data, not failure. Note it. Ask 'when do you think the last time was?' and let them sit with it.
  • Step 5 — Count the workarounds, not the wishes: When you tally interviews, count specific past-tense instances of the underlying behavior. If 8 out of 10 users had a workaround in the last 30 days, you have a feature. If 8 out of 10 said 'I would use it,' you have nothing.

Move 2 — The silence move

  • After a user finishes their first answer, **wait three to five seconds before saying anything**. Do not nod, do not 'mm-hmm', do not move on.

Traps to flag for the exam

The classic trap: leading questions disguised as innocent ones: Watch for sentences that start with **'Don't you think…?'** or **'Wouldn't it be nice if…?'** These are not questions. They are statements with a question mark stapled on. The user almost always agrees, because disagreeing is rude.

Takeaways (memorize these)

Users tell you what they want; their behavior tells you what they need. Tune for behavior, discount aspiration.
Replace every future-tense question with 'tell me about the last time…' — past tense produces stories, future tense produces fiction.
After the user's first answer, count to five before speaking. The second answer is the one you came for.
Workarounds are demand with a price tag attached; a side-built Google Sheet is the highest-signal artifact in B2B research.
If your question implies the answer you hope for, the answer is worthless. Test by flipping it — would the question feel just as natural with the opposite answer?
