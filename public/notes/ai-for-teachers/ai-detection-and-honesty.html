AI Detection vs AI Literacy

Stop policing the output; start designing the process

notes by Karthik ✏️

~ 13 min read ~

Every staff room in India is currently arguing the same fight. "We need a better AI detector." "Ban phones in homework." "Make them write in class." These are downstream symptoms of one upstream design failure: the assignment, as written, can be done by AI in 90 seconds.

Mental model: don't fight the output, design the process
When you write an assignment, you implicitly assume something about *how* a student will produce the answer. The traditional essay assumes 5 hours of solitary thinking. AI breaks that assumption; the answer can now be produced in 90 seconds with no thinking at all.

Move 1 — Assignments that require process

  • The first move is structural: rewrite the assignment so the *only* way to submit is to produce process artefacts. AI cannot produce these.
  • Step 1 — The vulnerable original: "Class 11 English: write a 600-word essay on whether technology has improved education in India. Submit Friday." 90-second AI job. Zero learning.
  • Step 2 — Add a local context anchor: "Begin with last week's class debate where Karan argued the opposite of what most of the class thought. Take Karan's side or rebut it; either way, name his actual argument." AI doesn't know what Karan said.
  • Step 3 — Add a draft requirement: "Submit a 200-word draft Monday with one paragraph and three rough notes for the others. Submit revised full draft Friday." Two artefacts. One missing = points off.
  • Step 4 — Add the process bibliography: "Append a 100-word note: 'what I tried first, what I changed, what I cut.' Honest answers welcome — saying 'I tried two terrible openings' is fine; saying 'I asked ChatGPT' is also fine if you say *what you did with* what it gave you." The note is itself the assessment of process.
  • Step 5 — Reserve a 5-minute oral check on Friday: "Random sample of 5 students will explain one of their paragraphs to me on submission day. Be ready to talk for 90 seconds." Just the *threat* of this changes behaviour.
  • Step 6 — Mark with the new rubric: Marks for: process artefacts present, local context engaged, voice consistent across drafts, oral defence credible. The final document is one of five things you grade, not the only thing.

Move 2 — The oral-defence move

  • The single most powerful tool in your honesty toolkit takes 5 minutes per student and costs nothing: **the oral defence.** Mechanic: pick a paragraph from the student's submission. Ask them to explain *why they made one specific edit, why they chose that example, what they considered and rejected*.

Traps to flag for the exam

The classic trap: false positives ruin a good student: The detector said 92%. You marked the zero. The student's parents come in. The student is your strongest English writer; her writing has always been formal and clean.

Takeaways (memorize these)

Detection is an arms race you cannot win; honesty design is one-time work that holds.
Stack two or more moves — drafts over time, local anchor, in-class write, process bibliography. Two layers is enough.
The 5-minute oral defence resolves 90% of suspicions. It tests ownership of edits, which AI cannot fake.
Detector scores are hints, never verdicts. Always have the conversation before the consequence touches a student's record.
False positives concentrate on your strongest writers. Marking zero on a probability score is how you damage a top student's year.
