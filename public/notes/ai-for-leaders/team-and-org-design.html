Team Topologies for AI

AI cell or AI factory? Pick the wrong one and the program dies in queue

notes by Meera ✏️

~ 13 min read ~

Six months in, your AI program has 14 ideas, 3 ships, and a 17-week backlog at the central AI team. Product managers have stopped bringing new ideas.

Mental model: AI cell vs AI factory
Two clean patterns; everything in between is debt.

Move 1 — The thin governance layer

  • If you build an AI factory, you also build a governance layer. The mistake is building it thick.
  • Step 1 — The English question: Board director: 'We need an AI ethics policy.' True. But 'AI ethics policy' decays into 47 pages and zero blocked launches without translation.
  • Step 2 — Pick what the policy actually blocks: Translate to: 'What concrete things should never reach a user?' Answer: model that fails > X% on the eval set, no red-team report, no named owner, no kill switch, no per-inference logging. Five things.
  • Step 3 — Pick the eval set: Name it: 'fintech-eval-v3, 1,200 prompts, last red-teamed 18 March 2026.' If the eval set does not exist, that is the first thing to build, not the policy doc.
  • Step 4 — Pick the override: One named human can override the threshold (e.g. CTO). The override produces a written log entry, automatically. No committee, no slide deck. Override is rare; record-keeping is mandatory.
  • Step 5 — Sign the page: CTO signs. CISO signs. Date stamped. Lives in version control. The 47-page policy doc, if it ever existed, is now an appendix that no one reads, and that is fine.

Move 2 — The 'where does the why live?' question

  • When you walk into a struggling AI program, ask one question: > *"Who, outside this AI team, can articulate the user problem this team is solving in one sentence, without me prompting?"* If the answer is 'no one' — the program is rudderless. The 'why' lives only in the AI team's slide deck, and slide decks are not strategy.

Traps to flag for the exam

The classic trap: centralise too early or decentralise too late: Both directions kill programs. Centralising before three concurrent product teams need the same capability turns the central team into a queue, and product teams stop bringing ideas. Decentralising past four concurrent product teams produces duplicated eval pipelines, inconsistent kill switches, and an audit that takes 14 weeks to reconstruct.

Takeaways (memorize these)

AI cell and AI factory are the only two clean structures; everything in between is debt.
Start as a cell; graduate to a factory when at least three concurrent product teams need the same capability.
Thin governance is a single page that blocks releases; thick policy is 47 pages that block nothing.
Where the 'why' lives tells you whether the program will survive a leadership change. Three external articulators, minimum.
Centralise too early and the council becomes a queue; decentralise too late and the audit becomes a scavenger hunt.
