Picking AI Bets That Won't Sink

Three bets, pre-committed kill criteria, one slot you can't fill yet

notes by Vikrant ✏️

~ 14 min read ~

Your VP of Product walks in with a 14-page deck for The Flagship AI Feature. It will cost ₹2.4 crore.

Mental model: a 3-bet portfolio with one slot you cannot yet fill
AI bets are not equities. Returns are not continuous; they are mostly binary at the 90-day mark. **Concentration is therefore worse, not better, than diversification.** One bet means one shot at learning; if it fails, you have spent a year and learned only that this team, on this problem, with this scope, did not work. Three independent bets give you three signals.

Move 1 — The hypothesis register

  • A hypothesis register is the single page that holds your AI program together. Every bet has one row.
  • Step 1 — The English question: VP of Operations: 'We want to use AI in claims processing.' The deck has 11 slides. The register has zero rows so far.
  • Step 2 — The bet column: Forced into one sentence: 'Pre-fill the claim form for motor claims under ₹50,000 in our top-3 distributor cities.' Notice what got cut: 'fully automate claims' was the original ask. The register said no.
  • Step 3 — The capability shift column: 'Multimodal OCR on damage photos passed 92% accuracy on our internal eval as of February 2026.' One phrase. If this column is hand-wavy, the bet is research, not a bet.
  • Step 4 — The cost ratio column: Manual coder ₹180/claim. LLM ₹4/claim. Ratio 45:1. Anything under ~5:1 should make you nervous; anything under 1:1 should kill the bet on the spot.
  • Step 5 — The kill criterion column: 'If auto-approval rate on the cohort is below 40% by 21 September 2026, sunset the pilot. Do not extend.' Cohort, threshold, action, date.
  • Step 6 — Owner and review: 'Rohan owns it. Reviewed 21 September.' Not 'the AI team.' Not 'Q3.' One human, one date.

Move 2 — The 90-day milestone gate

  • A 12-month AI roadmap is fiction. Capability shifts every quarter; vendor pricing shifts every month; your assumptions about user adoption are wrong almost every time.

Traps to flag for the exam

The classic trap: all-in on one flagship feature: If your AI program has exactly one flagship and your year is built around its launch, you have not built a portfolio; you have built a single point of failure with a budget. The flagship trap survives because it is *legible* — easier to explain to a board, easier to celebrate at all-hands, easier to put on a deck.

Takeaways (memorize these)

Concentration is worse than diversification for AI bets; binary 90-day outcomes punish single-bet programs.
The 3-bet portfolio with one reserved slot is the only structure that buys you a second cycle.
The hypothesis register is one page; empty cells are visible debt; the page is the program.
A 90-day kill criterion missed by 25% is missed; 'momentum' and 'next month's model' are the two phrases that should make you suspicious.
If a bet exceeds 50% of total program budget, you have a flagship in disguise; split it before the gate, not after.
