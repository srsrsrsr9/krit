Control Flow Without the Ceremony

if, for, while — and the 'where the cat sat on' off-by-one

notes by Vikrant ✏️

~ 14 min read ~

Most non-Python languages teach you to think of control flow as a series of conditional jumps. Python prefers to think of it as a *conversation* — *for each item in this collection*, do this.

Mental model: the staircase, not the elevator
Other languages give you elevators — `goto`, exceptions for control flow, complex switch fall-through. Python gives you a staircase. **You climb one step at a time, and the structure of your code matches the structure of the data**.

Move 1 — `for-each` over `for-i`

  • If you came from Java, C, or JavaScript-pre-2015, your fingers want to type `for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)`. In Python, this is almost always wrong.
  • 1. English: For each customer with revenue greater than 1000, send a thank-you email.
  • 2. The grain: We're iterating over customers. The unit of work is one customer.
  • 3. The filter: Skip customers below the threshold. Use a guard clause; avoid nesting.
  • 4. The action: Send the email.
  • 5. The compressed version: Same logic as a comprehension — useful when collecting results, not when only side-effecting.

Move 2 — The off-by-one trap (and how to never see it again)

  • Off-by-one bugs come in two shapes: 1. **Boundary confusion**: did you mean `< n` or `<= n`?

Takeaways (memorize these)

Iterate over items, not indices — `for x in items`, not `for i in range(len(items))`
Use `enumerate(items)` when you need both index and item; never maintain `i` manually
`zip(a, b)` walks two collections in lockstep; cleaner than parallel indexing
Python ranges and slices are stop-exclusive — `range(5)` is 0..4, `items[:5]` is the first 5
Comprehensions are for collecting results; never use them for side effects
