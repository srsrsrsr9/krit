Hiring for the AI Era

Execution went to zero; judgment is the only thing left to filter on

notes by Meera ✏️

~ 13 min read ~

Your hiring loop was designed for an era where execution was the moat. *Can you write the SQL?* *Can you draft the brief?* *Can you build the model?* The candidate who could execute fastest, cleanest, with the most polish, was the candidate to hire.

Mental model: hire for judgment, because execution went to zero
Hold this picture every time you walk into an interview. Execution skill — the ability to do the well-defined thing well — is now a commodity AI provides. Judgment — the ability to tell when a plausible answer is the wrong answer, when to push back, when to escalate, when to refuse — is the only thing left for a human to be senior at.

Move 1 — The new T-shape

  • The classical T-shape was a hiring metaphor invented at IDEO and spread across every tech company in the 2010s: broad surface knowledge across many areas, with deep expertise in one. It's still useful — but the *what* of the depth has changed.
  • Step 1 — Name the judgment moments: List 5 decisions a senior PM on this team made last quarter that an AI would not have made well. Be specific: 'said no to the founder's pet feature', 'caught the wrong dashboard metric before launch', 'pulled a deadline because engineering was hiding burnout'.
  • Step 2 — Convert each into a behavioral question: Each judgment moment becomes a 'tell me about a time' question. Avoid hypotheticals — those reward articulate liars. Past behavior is the only signal that ports.
  • Step 3 — Define the AI-supervisor probe: One question: 'Tell me about a time AI gave you a confident answer that turned out to be wrong. How did you catch it?' If they cannot answer, they have not actually used AI as a senior; they've used it as a junior.
  • Step 4 — Define the restraint probe: One question: 'Walk me through a decision where you chose not to use AI even though you could have.' The strongest seniors say no to AI sometimes. Those who never have, haven't been supervising it.
  • Step 5 — Set the rubric, calibrate the panel: Three levels per question: clear example with the failure mode named (4 points); plausible example without specifics (2 points); vague or absent (0 points). Calibrate the panel against three real candidates from the past quarter so the bar isn't drifting.

Move 2 — Behavioral interview design

  • Behavioral interviews work because past specific behavior predicts future behavior much better than hypothetical questions. *'Tell me about a time'* > *'what would you do if'*.

Traps to flag for the exam

The classic trap: hire for AI fluency, not for the actual job: The most common 2026 hiring mistake: a candidate's resume looks like an AI tool buffet, the recruiter is impressed, and the interview drifts into 'how do you use Cursor/Claude/Copilot' territory. Two rounds later, you've hired someone who can talk about AI but cannot actually do product strategy, financial analysis, or whatever the role is supposed to do.

Takeaways (memorize these)

Execution skill went to zero in 2024; judgment is the only thing left for a senior to be filtered on.
The new T-shape is broad-top, AI-orchestration band, deep-judgment-in-one-domain bottom.
The hiring squeeze is in the middle layer; mid-level pure-execution roles evaporate while senior judgment roles get more valuable.
Four behavioral questions cover most of what you need: the catch, the restraint, the hand-off, the disagreement.
'Hire for AI fluency, not the actual job' is the trap that fills your team with people who can talk about AI but cannot do the work.
