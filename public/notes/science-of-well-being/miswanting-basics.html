Why You Want the Wrong Things

Your brain is a wedding photographer with no memory card

notes by Aanya ✏️

~ 14 min read ~

You think you know what will make you happy. You are usually wrong.

Mental model: the wedding photographer with a broken memory card
Think of your brain as a wedding photographer with a card that only saves the *peak* and the *end* of every event. It then forecasts the future based on those two frames — and confidently tells you the trip will feel a certain way, the meeting will be excruciating, the new job will be life-changing. **The forecasts are systematically wrong, in a direction we can predict.**

Move 1 — Miswanting

  • **Miswanting** is wanting things that, on arrival, do not move you the way you predicted. The term was coined by Gilbert and Wilson; verified across decades on lottery winners, salary jumps, and yes, breakups.
  • Day 0 — The Want: You imagine the new salary, see yourself in a calmer apartment, sleeping deeper, finally relaxed. Predicted mood: 9 out of 10, permanent.
  • Day 3 — The Bump: Offer accepted. You text seven people. Your dopamine has, briefly, the chemistry of a small lottery win. Real mood: 8 out of 10.
  • Day 21 — The New Normal: You stop noticing the salary. You start noticing your manager's tone in standups. Mood: 7.4 out of 10.
  • Day 60 — The Comparison Reset: You learn what your peer at the next company makes. You feel poorer than you did three months ago, on a higher salary. Mood: 6.9 out of 10.
  • Day 90 — Baseline: Same mood as before, slightly more tax, more meetings. The thing you wanted did exactly what the data said it would do. Mood: 7.0 out of 10.

Move 2 — The Arrival Fallacy

  • The **arrival fallacy** is miswanting with a finish line. *"Once I crack the JEE, life starts."* *"Once I hit FIRE, I will relax."* *"Once the wedding is over, we will breathe."* Tal Ben-Shahar named it after winning his own squash championship: he won, expected ecstasy, got about 11 minutes of it, then went looking for the next finish line.

Traps to flag for the exam

The classic trap: the finish-line stack: You do not have one arrival fallacy; you have a **queue** of them. Promotion, then house, then car, then second house, then kid's school admission, then *their* JEE. Each arrival quietly hands the baton to the next, and you mistake the running for progress.

Takeaways (memorize these)

Your brain is a wedding photographer that only saves the peak and the end frames
Miswanting is a prediction error, not a desire problem
The arrival fallacy installs a new finish line the moment you cross the old one
Bumps from external events fade in 2 to 12 weeks; nothing in the dataset disagrees
The diagnostic question is: what does my Tuesday afternoon look like 90 days after I get this thing?
